Seeing the pattern before you solve

Most of these problems are one of seven types. Each type has a visual cue. Spot the cue, and the method follows.

The checklist

Run through these questions in order when you first look at a problem.

  1. Is it only arithmetic? Convert mixed numbers and follow order of operations.
  2. Is there a number in front of parentheses? Distribute first; the numbers are usually built to cancel.
  3. Is x in a denominator? Factor, multiply by the LCD, then check excluded values.
  4. Does a chunk repeat, or appear next to its own reciprocal? Substitute a single letter for it.
  5. Does it go on forever (“…”)? The tail equals the whole, so replace it with x.
  6. After clearing fractions, is it linear or quadratic? Solve accordingly.

The seven patterns

Linear equations with number denominators

Cue: x appears only on top; every denominator is a plain number.

Method: Multiply every term by the LCD — the lowest common denominator, meaning the smallest number every denominator divides into evenly. For 3 and 6 that is 6; for 6, 15 and 10 it is 30. Each fraction then cancels down to a whole number and you are left with ordinary linear algebra.

A minus in front of a fraction hits the whole numerator: −(2 − x) becomes −2 + x.

Worked example: 05 #1.

Distribution with “scary” coefficients

Cue: Ugly fractions like \(\tfrac{5}{13}\) sit in front of parentheses.

Method: Distribute before doing anything else. The numbers are chosen so the denominator cancels against what is inside the parentheses:

\[\tfrac{5}{13}(26x+39)=\tfrac{5\cdot26}{13}x+\tfrac{5\cdot39}{13}=10x+15\]

because \(26\div13=2\) and \(39\div13=3\). Whole numbers coming out clean means you are on the intended path; a leftover fraction usually means an arithmetic slip.

Worked example: 05 #2.

Arithmetic and order of operations

Cue: Mixed numbers, or a string of ÷, ·, +, −.

Method: A mixed number becomes improper by multiplying the whole part by the denominator and adding the numerator: \(2\tfrac34=\frac{2\cdot4+3}{4}=\frac{11}{4}\).

For a stack with one long fraction bar, simplify the top and the bottom separately, then divide by multiplying by the reciprocal of the bottom.

For a string of operations with no fraction bar, do ÷ and · first, then + and −, working left to right within each stage.

Worked examples: 05 #3 and #8.

x in the denominator

Cue: Expressions like \(x-2\) or \(x^2-4\) below the fraction bar.

Method: Factor every denominator first. Differences of squares are the usual trick: \(x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)\).

The LCD is then the product of the distinct factors you can see. With \(x-2\), \(x+2\) and \((x-2)(x+2)\) on the three denominators, the LCD is just \((x-2)(x+2)\) — the third one already contains the other two. Multiply both sides by it and every denominator cancels, leaving a linear or quadratic equation.

Write the excluded values down before you multiply — any x making an original denominator zero. Multiplying hides them, so a solution that matches one gets thrown out.

Worked examples: 05 #6 and QR2 #7.

Substitution

Cue: The same chunk appears twice, especially an expression and its reciprocal:

\[\frac{x+1}{x-3} = -4 + 21\left(\frac{x-3}{x+1}\right)\]

Method: Name the chunk. Let \(u=\frac{x+1}{x-3}\). The fraction on the right is its reciprocal, so it becomes \(\frac1u\), and the whole equation collapses to something small:

\[u=-4+\frac{21}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; u^2+4u-21=0\]

Solve that for \(u\), then turn each value of \(u\) back into an equation in \(x\) and solve again. Two values of \(u\) give two equations, and so two answers.

Substitution shows up elsewhere in algebra on the same cue — \(x^4\) alongside \(x^2\) (use \(u=x^2\)), \((x+1)^2\) alongside \((x+1)\), \(\sqrt{x}\) alongside \(x\) — though none of those appear on these two sheets.

Substitution is a shortcut, not a requirement. Clearing denominators works too, just with more room for arithmetic mistakes.

Worked example: QR2 #8.

Infinite continued fractions

Cue: A fraction whose own denominator contains a copy of itself, trailing off in “…”:

\[x=4+\cfrac{5}{4+\cfrac{5}{4+\cdots}}\]

Method: Everything below that first fraction bar is the same endless expression you started with, which is \(x\). Replace it with \(x\) and the tower collapses to one line:

\[x=4+\frac{5}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; x^2=4x+5 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2-4x-5=0\]

Multiplying through by \(x\) is what turns it into a quadratic.

Every number in the original is positive, so x cannot come out negative. Solve the quadratic, then reject the negative root.

The tower above is QR2 #5. Worked examples: QR2 #5 and 05 #5, which uses 3 and 18 in place of 4 and 5. QR2 #3 looks similar but is finite — just clear the fractions.

Quadratics

Cue: An \(x^2\) term with no x left in any denominator — either handed to you that way, or arrived at after clearing fractions.

Method: Move everything to one side and make the leading coefficient positive, multiplying every term by \(-1\) if it isn’t. Read off \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) with their signs, then check the discriminant \(b^2-4ac\): a perfect square means it factors, anything else means the formula.

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\]

Two steps always follow the formula. Simplify the radical by splitting out the largest perfect square — \(\sqrt{208}=\sqrt{16\cdot13}=4\sqrt{13}\) — and then reduce by dividing every term, top and bottom, by any common factor.

−4ac turns positive whenever c is negative. That sign flip is the most common mistake on these.

Worked examples: 05 #10 and #11, plus the last step of several problems above.

05 #4x in denominator
\[\frac{3x}{4}+\frac{x-2}{x+2}=\frac{1}{2}\]
Solution

Cue: x appears in a denominator: \(x+2\).

Method: Note the excluded value, multiply by the LCD \(4(x+2)\), then solve the quadratic.

Excluded value: \(x\neq-2\). Multiply by the LCD \(4(x+2)\):

\[3x(x+2)+4(x-2)=2(x+2)\]
\[3x^2+10x-8=2x+4 \;\Rightarrow\; 3x^2+8x-12=0\]

Identify a, b, c. \(a=3\), \(b=8\), \(c=-12\). The sign belongs to c.

Compute the discriminant, \(b^2-4ac\):

\[b^2-4ac = 8^2-4(3)(-12) = 64-(-144) = 64+144 = 208\]

c is negative, so −4ac turns into +144. This sign flip is the most common mistake.

208 is between \(14^2=196\) and \(15^2=225\), so it isn’t a perfect square. The quadratic won’t factor; use the full formula:

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}=\frac{-8\pm\sqrt{208}}{2(3)}=\frac{-8\pm\sqrt{208}}{6}\]

Simplify the root. The largest perfect square dividing 208 is 16: \(208=16\cdot13\), so \(\sqrt{208}=4\sqrt{13}\).

\[x=\frac{-8\pm4\sqrt{13}}{6}\]

Reduce. Divide every term (−8, 4, and 6) by 2, not just the −8:

\(x=\dfrac{-4\pm2\sqrt{13}}{3}\) (about 1.07 and −3.74)

Every problem on these sheets is built from a handful of small tools. Most of the work is choosing which ones, and in what order.

The checklist

Almost every one of these problems is “find a slope, find a point, write the equation.” Work through in order.

  1. Is the line horizontal or vertical? Stop here. Horizontal is \(y=\) the y-coordinate; vertical is \(x=\) the x-coordinate.
  2. What slope do you have? Given outright, or from two points \(m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\), or from an equation \(Ax+By=C\) as \(m=-\frac{A}{B}\).
  3. Does it say perpendicular or parallel? Perpendicular flips the slope and changes its sign. Parallel keeps it.
  4. What point do you have? If none is given outright, the problem is telling you to build one: a midpoint, a point that partitions a segment, or the intersection of two lines.
  5. Write point-slope: \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\). Always start here once you have a slope and a point.
  6. Was standard form asked for? Multiply through by the denominator of the slope, move the x term left, and make A positive.
  7. Was a distance asked for? Two points go straight into the distance formula. A point and a line need the perpendicular built first.
  8. Is there a letter where a number should be? Carry it through the same steps, write the condition (perpendicular, parallel, a distance, a ratio, on the line) as an equation, and solve for the letter.

A ratio like 7:12 or 5:4 is the signal to build a point, not to divide anything yet.

The eleven patterns

Slope from two points

Cue: Two coordinate pairs and no slope given.

Method: \(m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\), kept as a fraction. Subtract in the same order top and bottom.

“Passes through the origin” hands you a free second point, \((0,0)\).

Run first, then rise: 4 over 6, so m = 2/3. Go the other way and both flip sign — −4 over −6 — which is the same fraction. That is why the order only has to match.

Worked example: GL2 #2.

Perpendicular and parallel slopes

Cue: The words perpendicular or parallel, or the \(\perp\) symbol.

Method: Perpendicular flips the fraction and changes the sign. \(-\frac52\to\frac25\), \(3\to-\frac13\), \(1\to-1\). Parallel keeps the same slope.

To pull a slope out of \(Ax+By=C\), either solve for \(y\) or use \(m=-\frac{A}{B}\) directly.

The same triangle turned a quarter turn: over 3 / up 2 becomes over 2 / up 3, and “over” now points the other way. Swapping the legs is the flip; the change of direction is the minus sign.

You only need the slope of the other line, so its constant is decoration. In GL2 #4 the 32.9 never gets used.

One pair the flip-and-negate rule cannot express: a horizontal line (slope 0) and a vertical line (no slope) are perpendicular, but 0 has no reciprocal. Handle those by sight.

Worked examples: GL2 #4, RD04 CG; Basics #1 and #6 and Review #5, #6 and #9 with a letter in the slope.

The three forms

Cue: “Write in standard form,” or an answer blank labeled Standard.

\[y-y_1=m(x-x_1) \qquad y=mx+b \qquad Ax+By=C\]

Method: Which form you start in depends on what you were handed. A slope with the y-intercept drops straight into \(y=mx+b\). A slope with any other point goes into point-slope, which is the workbench: you can write it the moment you have a slope and a point.

From either one, to reach standard form: multiply both sides by the slope’s denominator, move the x term to the left, and if A came out negative multiply every term by \(-1\).

Standard form means A positive, and no fractions or decimals anywhere.

Worked example: GL2 #1.

Midpoint

Cue: “Midpoint,” “bisects,” or “perpendicular bisector.”

\[M=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\]

Method: Average each coordinate. A perpendicular bisector is just two patterns stacked: negative reciprocal for the slope, midpoint for the point.

Worked examples: GL2 #5, FT05 #2.

Partitioning a segment by a ratio

Cue: A point placed along a segment by a ratio, as in “Q lies on \(\overline{AB}\) such that \(AQ:AB=7:12\).”

Method: Find the run and rise of the whole segment, scale both by the same fraction, and add them to the starting point.

Which fraction? That depends on what the ratio is comparing, and there are two kinds. Check whether the second name in the ratio is the whole segment or just another piece of it.

Part to whole. In \(AQ:AB=7:12\), AB is the entire segment, so the 12 already counts the whole thing. Q sits \(\frac{7}{12}\) of the way from A, and \(\frac{7}{12}\) is the number you scale by.

Part to part. In \(JP:PK=5:4\), both JP and PK are pieces, and neither is the whole. Add them to get the whole: \(5+4=9\) parts. So P sits \(\frac59\) of the way from J to K — and if you already know J and P and want K, the step from P to K is \(\frac45\) of the step from J to P, because PK is 4 parts to JP’s 5.

A ratio to the whole segment and a ratio between two pieces look almost identical. AB is the whole; PK is not.

Same idea, different denominator: the 12 is handed to you, the 9 you have to build from 5 + 4.

Written as a proportion. The solved problems set this up the way the teacher does: draw the right triangle on the segment, call the small triangle’s legs \(m\) and \(n\), and write small leg over big leg equals the ratio, as in \(\frac{n}{36}=\frac{7}{12}\). Solving that proportion is the same arithmetic as scaling by \(\frac{7}{12}\).

Distance between two points

Cue: Two points and the word distance, or a length you need before anything else can be answered.

\[D=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\]

It is the Pythagorean theorem. The two differences are the legs, the distance is the hypotenuse, and \(a^2+b^2=c^2\) is the formula.

Method: Squaring removes the signs, so subtraction order doesn’t matter here — unlike in the slope formula.

Then simplify the radical: split the number into a perfect square times whatever is left over, and take the root of the square. \(\sqrt{50}=\sqrt{25\cdot2}=5\sqrt2\).

Distance from a point to a line

Cue: A point and a line, and the word distance.

This is the four-step one. Distance means the shortest distance, which runs perpendicular to the line, so you have to find where that perpendicular lands before you can measure anything.

  1. Take the slope of the given line and flip it to the perpendicular slope.
  2. Write the perpendicular line through the given point.
  3. Set the two equations equal and solve. That x, with its y, is the foot of the perpendicular.
  4. Distance formula from the original point to the foot.

Every other route from A to the line is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose leg is the red segment, so it can only be longer. That is why the perpendicular has to be built before anything can be measured.

The foot is nearly always an ugly fraction. Keep it as a fraction — decimals will wreck the radical at the end.

Why the answers come out looking like \(\frac{23\sqrt{13}}{13}\). The foot’s two coordinates share a denominator — 13 in DPL #1, 41 in the other three — so both differences do too. Squaring turns that denominator into a perfect square, and the root of a perfect square is a whole number:

\[\sqrt{\frac{6877}{169}}=\frac{\sqrt{6877}}{13}=\frac{23\sqrt{13}}{13}\]

So the bottom is left with no radical on it and the answer is already rationalized. Nothing further is needed.

Worked examples: all four of DPL, Basics #3, Review #1 and #2.

Intercepts

Cue: The words x-intercept or y-intercept, either as given points or as the thing to find.

Method: An intercept is where the line crosses an axis, and on an axis the other coordinate is zero. For the x-intercept set \(y=0\); for the y-intercept set \(x=0\).

\[x-2y=10:\qquad y=0\Rightarrow x=10,\ \ (10,0) \qquad\qquad x=0\Rightarrow -2y=10,\ \ (0,-5)\]

The x-intercept has y = 0, not x = 0. Name the axis it sits on, then zero the other letter.

If the intercepts are unknown but related, name one with a letter. “The x-intercept is 5 times the y-intercept” gives \((0,A)\) and \((5A,0)\), and the slope between them, \(-\frac{A}{5A}=-\frac15\), comes out without needing A.

A triangle with two corners on the x-axis has its base along the axis (subtract the two x-intercepts) and its height equal to the third corner’s y-coordinate.

Two lines crossing

Cue: The word intersection, or one point that has to satisfy two equations at once.

Method: When both lines are in standard form, use elimination: multiply one equation so a variable has opposite coefficients, then add the equations. That variable disappears and you solve for the other.

\[\begin{aligned}4x-9y&=2\\2x+3y&=-14\ \ (\times3)\end{aligned}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\begin{aligned}4x-9y&=2\\6x+9y&=-42\end{aligned}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 10x=-40\]

Step 3 of the point-to-line method is this same job. There both lines are already solved for \(y\), so setting the right sides equal is quicker.

Factoring is a sorting job. Pull out what every term shares, count what’s left, and the count tells you which pattern to try.

The checklist

Every problem on these sheets follows the same order. Run through it each time, and run it again on every factor you produce. The Patterns tab ends with the chains these steps usually form.

  1. Tidy up. If the problem is written in pieces that don’t share a factor, distribute and combine first. Then put the terms in descending powers.
  2. GCF first, always. Pull out the biggest number and the smallest power of each letter. If the leading term is negative, pull out the negative too, which flips every sign inside.
  3. Count the terms.
    • Two terms: a difference of squares, or a sum or difference of cubes. A sum of two squares doesn’t factor.
    • Three terms: a perfect square? A leading 1 (product and sum)? Otherwise the AC method. If the exponents are like \(x^4\) and \(x^2\), treat \(x^2\) as the variable.
    • Four or more terms: group in pairs. If the pairs don’t share a binomial, re-order so a difference of squares or cubes sits in one pair. Groups don’t have to be pairs: a trinomial plus a pair, or three pairs, work the same way.
  4. Two binomials that are almost the same? \(1-w\) and \(w-1\) are opposites; pull out −1 to flip one. \(j+3m\) and \(3m+j\) are already equal.
  5. Almost a perfect square, but the middle is wrong? Force-factor: add and subtract what’s missing, then it becomes a difference of squares.
  6. Look at every factor again. \(x^4-1\) gives \(x^2-1\), which factors again. Stop only when nothing left will factor.
  7. Check by multiplying back. You should get the original problem.

After pulling out a GCF, the leftover terms almost always factor nicely. If they don’t, recheck the GCF.

The ten patterns

Tidy up and pull out the GCF

Cue: Every term shares a number or a letter; terms are out of order; a negative leading term; or the problem is written as pieces that don’t share a factor.

Method: Re-order into descending powers, then pull out the GCF: the biggest number dividing every coefficient, times the smallest power of each letter that appears in every term.

\[25x^5y^4-5x^3y^4+25x^3y^3=5x^3y^3(5x^2y-y+5)\]

When the leading term is negative, pull out a negative. Every sign inside flips: \(-x^3+3x^2+4x=-x(x^2-3x-4)\).

If the pieces don’t share a factor, as in \(x(x+4)-4(1+x)\), multiply out and combine before trying anything else. That one collapses to \(x^2-4\).

Fractions inside the pieces are a hint too. In \(9\left(x^2+\frac{x}{3}\right)+3\left(x+\frac13\right)\) the outside numbers cancel them: it becomes \(9x^2+6x+1\).

Worked examples: F02 #12, #13, #20, #23; F03 #18, #19, #21; RD01 #1, #11; RD02 #1; RD04 #2, #3; FT05 #5, #10.

Trinomials with a leading 1

Cue: \(x^2+bx+c\).

Method: Two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\). They go straight into the parentheses with their signs.

\[x^2-7x-18:\quad -9\cdot2=-18,\ \ -9+2=-7 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (x-9)(x+2)\]

Signs tell you where to look. If \(c\) is positive, both numbers have the sign of \(b\). If \(c\) is negative, one is positive and one negative, and the bigger one has the sign of \(b\).

A second letter just rides along: \(x^2+12xy-45y^2=(x+15y)(x-3y)\).

Worked examples: F02 #16–19; F03 #11, #12, #23; the last step of RD02 #6 and #9, RD04 #9 and FT05 #7.

The AC method

Cue: A leading coefficient that isn’t 1, after any GCF is out.

Method: Multiply \(a\cdot c\). Find two numbers that multiply to \(a\cdot c\) and add to \(b\). Use them to split the middle term into two, then group the four terms in pairs.

The X as the keys draw it, for RD01 #4, 3x² + 4x − 175. The circled numbers multiply to the right side and add to the left side: 25 · (−21) = −525 and 25 + (−21) = 4.

\[3x^2+25x-21x-175=x(3x+25)-7(3x+25)=(3x+25)(x-7)\]

Both pairs must show the same binomial. If they don’t, a sign is off. Usually the second pair’s GCF needs to be negative.

Worked examples: RD01 #1 and #4; RD02 #1, #3, #5; RD04 #2; FT05 #6, #9.

Perfect square trinomials

Cue: First and last terms are perfect squares, and the middle is twice the product of their roots.

\[a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2 \qquad a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2\]

(3x + 2)² drawn as a square. The two corner squares are the first and last terms. The two matching rectangles are why the middle term is 2 times the product: 6x + 6x = 12x.

Method: Write the square root under the first and last terms. If 2 times their product matches the middle, it is a perfect square, and the factor is the two roots joined by the middle term’s sign.

Difference of squares

Cue: Two perfect squares with a minus between them.

\[a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)\]

Method: Find what is being squared on each side. Powers count: \(x^4=(x^2)^2\), \(64y^4=(8y^2)^2\).

Then check the minus factor again. \(x^4-81=(x^2+9)(x^2-9)\), and \(x^2-9\) is itself a difference of squares, so the answer is \((x^2+9)(x+3)(x-3)\).

A sum of two squares, like x² + 9, doesn’t factor. Leave it.

Exponents halve each time: \(x^{16}-81=(x^8+9)(x^8-9)=(x^8+9)(x^4+3)(x^4-3)\). Halve the exponent, but take the square root of the number. \(x^4-3\) stops, because 3 isn’t a perfect square.

Worked examples: F02 #25, #29; F03 #15, #25, #29; RD02 #5; FT05 #8.

Sum and difference of cubes

Cue: Two perfect cubes. Know these: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125. Exponents that are multiples of 3 count too: \(z^9=(z^3)^3\).

\[a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) \qquad a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)\]

Method: Write each term as something cubed, then fill in the pattern. The first factor copies the original sign. The middle term of the second factor takes the opposite sign, and its last term is always plus.

The second factor never factors further. Don’t try to split x² + 2x + 4.

A whole binomial can be the thing that’s cubed. In \((2x+1)^3+(2x-1)^3\), use \(a=2x+1\) and \(b=2x-1\), then simplify inside each bracket.

Worked examples: F02 #30; F03 #20, #30; RD01 #6, #12; RD02 #7, #9; RD04 #4, #5, #9; FT05 #5, #6.

Quadratics in disguise

Cue: Three terms where the first exponent is double the middle one: \(x^4\) and \(x^2\), or \(x^8\) and \(x^4\).

Method: Factor as if the middle power were \(x\). Then every factor that’s a difference of squares gets factored again.

\[x^4-10x^2+9=(x^2-1)(x^2-9)=(x+1)(x-1)(x+3)(x-3)\]

The same trick works when the repeated piece is a whole chunk. \(a^2+2ab+b^2+17a+17b+16\) is \((a+b)^2+17(a+b)+16\); let \(u=a+b\), factor \(u^2+17u+16\), then put \(a+b\) back.

Worked examples: F02 #11, #15; F03 #8; RD01 #3; RD02 #5; FT05 #7.

Grouping

Cue: Four terms.

Method: Split into two pairs and take the GCF of each pair. If both pairs leave the same binomial, pull that binomial out.

\[2x-2xm+a-am=2x(1-m)+a(1-m)=(1-m)(2x+a)\]

Sometimes a binomial is already visible, as in \(x^2(a-1)+x(a-1)-42a+42\). Make the leftover terms show it too: \(-42a+42=-42(a-1)\).

Groups don’t have to be pairs. Six terms can make three pairs that all leave the same binomial, and five terms can be a trinomial plus a pair: \(x^2+5x+6-xy-2y=(x+3)(x+2)-y(x+2)\).

If the two binomials are opposites, like \((1-w)\) and \((w-1)\), pull out −1 from one: \(-2(w-1)=2(1-w)\).

Worked examples: F02 #26, #31; F03 #26, #31; RD01 #8, #10; RD02 #6, #7, #8; RD04 #1, #5, #8; FT05 #5, #6, #9.

Grouping with a special pair

Cue: Four terms where simple pairing fails, but two of the terms are squares or cubes. The sheet’s hint is “do a bit of re-ordering.”

Method: Put the two squares (or cubes) together and factor that pair with its pattern. Take the GCF of the other pair. The binomial from the pattern shows up in both.

\[x^3+3x^2-6x-8=\underbrace{x^3-8}_{\text{cubes}}+\underbrace{3x^2-6x}_{\text{GCF}}=(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)+3x(x-2)\]

Three terms can make the special group too. In \(x^2-y^2+10x+25\), the terms \(x^2+10x+25\) are a perfect square, which leaves \((x+5)^2-y^2\), a difference of squares.

Worked examples: F03 #22; RD01 #2, #5, #6, #12; RD02 #2, #4, #9; RD04 #6, #7, #9.

Force-factoring

Cue: First and last terms are perfect squares, but the middle isn’t 2 times their product. Or a sum like \(x^4+4\) that seems stuck.

Method: Work out the middle term a perfect square would have. Add and subtract the difference, so the value doesn’t change. The perfect square factors, and what’s left is a difference of squares.

\[x^4+4x^2+16=x^4+8x^2+16-4x^2=(x^2+4)^2-(2x)^2\]
\[=(x^2+4+2x)(x^2+4-2x)\]

This only works when the amount you subtract is itself a perfect square, like 4x² = (2x)².

Pick the sign of the target middle so the leftover gets subtracted. For \(x^4-9x^2+64\), aim for \(+16x^2\) (add \(25x^2\), subtract it back), not \(-16x^2\), which would leave a sum.

Worked examples: RD01 #7, #9, #10; RD04 #1. FT05 #8 shows when it can’t work.

Which terms to pair

When four or more terms don’t pair up as written, try these in order:

  1. Squares together, or cubes together. \(25x^2\) with \(49y^2\); \(x^3\) with \(8\).
  2. Three terms that make a perfect square. \(x^2+26x+169\) goes together, and the leftover \(-4y^2\) stands alone.
  3. Terms that share a letter. In \(3m^2+2nx+mx+6mn\), pair the two terms with \(m\) and no \(n\), then the two with \(n\).

Test the pairing straight away: take the GCF of each group. If they leave the same binomial, keep going. If not, swap partners before doing anything else.

Which sign to pull out

The second group’s GCF must leave the same binomial as the first. If you get \((3y-x)\) from one pair and \((x-3y)\) from the other, you pulled out the wrong sign. Take the negative instead and they match.

\[9y^3-3xy^2+3x^3-9x^2y=3y^2(3y-x)\,\underline{-3x^2}(3y-x)\]

The same idea fixes binomials that come out as opposites from different patterns: \(w-1=-(1-w)\).

Examples: RD01 #2, #8; RD02 #2; RD04 #6; FT05 #6, #9.

When to stop

  • A sum of two squares stops (\(x^2+9\), \(4a^2+b^2\)), with one exception: force-factoring. \(x^4+c^2\) splits only if the middle you’d need, \(2cx^2\), is a perfect square. \(x^4+4\) needs \(4x^2\) ✓, \(x^4+64\) needs \(16x^2\) ✓, but \(x^4+16\) needs \(8x^2\), and 8 isn’t a square, so it stops.
  • A difference stops if either side isn’t a perfect square: \(x^2-2\), \(x^4-3\), \(2x^4-1\). Cubes are the same: \(3x^3-1\) stops because 3 isn’t a cube.
  • The second factor from a sum or difference of cubes never factors.
  • A trinomial stops when no two numbers work. For \(x^2+6x+6\), nothing multiplies to 6 and adds to 6.

Check the degree. Add up the highest power in each factor (count \(x^3\) as 3, a squared factor twice). It must equal the highest power in the original. That one check catches the key’s slip in RD04 #5.

Method: Difference of squares, then check every factor again.

\[(x^8+9)(x^8-9)\]

\(x^8-9\) is another difference of squares: \((x^4)^2-3^2\).

\[(x^8+9)(x^4+3)(x^4-3)\]

\((x^8+9)(x^4+3)(x^4-3)\)

FT05 #9Grouping in threes, then AC

Factor completely: \(4kx^2-16x^2+4kx-16x-15k+60\)

Solution

Cue: Six terms that fall into three pairs, each with a \(k\) term and a plain term.

Method: GCF of each pair. All three leave \((k-4)\); pull it out and factor what’s left.

GCF of each pair: \(4x^2\), \(4x\), and \(-15\).

\[4x^2(k-4)+4x(k-4)-15(k-4)\]
\[(k-4)(4x^2+4x-15)\]

\[(k-4)(4x^2+10x-6x-15)\]
\[(k-4)\left[2x(2x+5)-3(2x+5)\right]\]
\((k-4)(2x+5)(2x-3)\)

Taking out −15, not 15, is what makes the last pair come out as (k − 4).
