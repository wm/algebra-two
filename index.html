Seeing the pattern before you solve

Most of these problems are one of seven types. Each type has a visual cue. Spot the cue, and the method follows.

The checklist

Run through these questions in order when you first look at a problem.

  1. Is it only arithmetic? Convert mixed numbers and follow order of operations.
  2. Is there a number in front of parentheses? Distribute first; the numbers are usually built to cancel.
  3. Is x in a denominator? Factor, multiply by the LCD, then check excluded values.
  4. Does a chunk repeat, or appear next to its own reciprocal? Substitute a single letter for it.
  5. Does it go on forever (“…”)? The tail equals the whole, so replace it with x.
  6. After clearing fractions, is it linear or quadratic? Solve accordingly.

The seven patterns

Linear equations with number denominators

Cue: x appears only on top; every denominator is a plain number.

Method: Multiply every term by the LCD — the lowest common denominator, meaning the smallest number every denominator divides into evenly. For 3 and 6 that is 6; for 6, 15 and 10 it is 30. Each fraction then cancels down to a whole number and you are left with ordinary linear algebra.

A minus in front of a fraction hits the whole numerator: −(2 − x) becomes −2 + x.

Worked example: 05 #1.

Distribution with “scary” coefficients

Cue: Ugly fractions like \(\tfrac{5}{13}\) sit in front of parentheses.

Method: Distribute before doing anything else. The numbers are chosen so the denominator cancels against what is inside the parentheses:

\[\tfrac{5}{13}(26x+39)=\tfrac{5\cdot26}{13}x+\tfrac{5\cdot39}{13}=10x+15\]

because \(26\div13=2\) and \(39\div13=3\). Whole numbers coming out clean means you are on the intended path; a leftover fraction usually means an arithmetic slip.

Worked example: 05 #2.

Arithmetic and order of operations

Cue: Mixed numbers, or a string of ÷, ·, +, −.

Method: A mixed number becomes improper by multiplying the whole part by the denominator and adding the numerator: \(2\tfrac34=\frac{2\cdot4+3}{4}=\frac{11}{4}\).

For a stack with one long fraction bar, simplify the top and the bottom separately, then divide by multiplying by the reciprocal of the bottom.

For a string of operations with no fraction bar, do ÷ and · first, then + and −, working left to right within each stage.

Worked examples: 05 #3 and #8.

x in the denominator

Cue: Expressions like \(x-2\) or \(x^2-4\) below the fraction bar.

Method: Factor every denominator first. Differences of squares are the usual trick: \(x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)\).

The LCD is then the product of the distinct factors you can see. With \(x-2\), \(x+2\) and \((x-2)(x+2)\) on the three denominators, the LCD is just \((x-2)(x+2)\) — the third one already contains the other two. Multiply both sides by it and every denominator cancels, leaving a linear or quadratic equation.

Write the excluded values down before you multiply — any x making an original denominator zero. Multiplying hides them, so a solution that matches one gets thrown out.

Worked examples: 05 #6 and QR2 #7.

Substitution

Cue: The same chunk appears twice, especially an expression and its reciprocal:

\[\frac{x+1}{x-3} = -4 + 21\left(\frac{x-3}{x+1}\right)\]

Method: Name the chunk. Let \(u=\frac{x+1}{x-3}\). The fraction on the right is its reciprocal, so it becomes \(\frac1u\), and the whole equation collapses to something small:

\[u=-4+\frac{21}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; u^2+4u-21=0\]

Solve that for \(u\), then turn each value of \(u\) back into an equation in \(x\) and solve again. Two values of \(u\) give two equations, and so two answers.

Substitution shows up elsewhere in algebra on the same cue — \(x^4\) alongside \(x^2\) (use \(u=x^2\)), \((x+1)^2\) alongside \((x+1)\), \(\sqrt{x}\) alongside \(x\) — though none of those appear on these two sheets.

Substitution is a shortcut, not a requirement. Clearing denominators works too, just with more room for arithmetic mistakes.

Worked example: QR2 #8.

Infinite continued fractions

Cue: A fraction whose own denominator contains a copy of itself, trailing off in “…”:

\[x=4+\cfrac{5}{4+\cfrac{5}{4+\cdots}}\]

Method: Everything below that first fraction bar is the same endless expression you started with, which is \(x\). Replace it with \(x\) and the tower collapses to one line:

\[x=4+\frac{5}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; x^2=4x+5 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2-4x-5=0\]

Multiplying through by \(x\) is what turns it into a quadratic.

Every number in the original is positive, so x cannot come out negative. Solve the quadratic, then reject the negative root.

The tower above is QR2 #5. Worked examples: QR2 #5 and 05 #5, which uses 3 and 18 in place of 4 and 5. QR2 #3 looks similar but is finite — just clear the fractions.

Quadratics

Cue: An \(x^2\) term with no x left in any denominator — either handed to you that way, or arrived at after clearing fractions.

Method: Move everything to one side and make the leading coefficient positive, multiplying every term by \(-1\) if it isn’t. Read off \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) with their signs, then check the discriminant \(b^2-4ac\): a perfect square means it factors, anything else means the formula.

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\]

Two steps always follow the formula. Simplify the radical by splitting out the largest perfect square — \(\sqrt{208}=\sqrt{16\cdot13}=4\sqrt{13}\) — and then reduce by dividing every term, top and bottom, by any common factor.

−4ac turns positive whenever c is negative. That sign flip is the most common mistake on these.

Worked examples: 05 #10 and #11, plus the last step of several problems above.

05 #4x in denominator
\[\frac{3x}{4}+\frac{x-2}{x+2}=\frac{1}{2}\]
Solution

Cue: x appears in a denominator: \(x+2\).

Method: Note the excluded value, multiply by the LCD \(4(x+2)\), then solve the quadratic.

Excluded value: \(x\neq-2\). Multiply by the LCD \(4(x+2)\):

\[3x(x+2)+4(x-2)=2(x+2)\]
\[3x^2+10x-8=2x+4 \;\Rightarrow\; 3x^2+8x-12=0\]

Identify a, b, c. \(a=3\), \(b=8\), \(c=-12\). The sign belongs to c.

Compute the discriminant, \(b^2-4ac\):

\[b^2-4ac = 8^2-4(3)(-12) = 64-(-144) = 64+144 = 208\]

c is negative, so −4ac turns into +144. This sign flip is the most common mistake.

208 is between \(14^2=196\) and \(15^2=225\), so it isn’t a perfect square. The quadratic won’t factor; use the full formula:

\[x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}=\frac{-8\pm\sqrt{208}}{2(3)}=\frac{-8\pm\sqrt{208}}{6}\]

Simplify the root. The largest perfect square dividing 208 is 16: \(208=16\cdot13\), so \(\sqrt{208}=4\sqrt{13}\).

\[x=\frac{-8\pm4\sqrt{13}}{6}\]

Reduce. Divide every term (−8, 4, and 6) by 2, not just the −8:

\(x=\dfrac{-4\pm2\sqrt{13}}{3}\) (about 1.07 and −3.74)

05 #9Substitution
\[7+\frac{x+4}{x-2}=2-4\left(\frac{x-2}{x+4}\right)\]
Solution

Cue: \(\tfrac{x+4}{x-2}\) and \(\tfrac{x-2}{x+4}\) are reciprocals of each other.

Method: Substitute \(u=\tfrac{x+4}{x-2}\), solve the quadratic in u, then solve for x from each u.

Excluded values: \(x\neq2,\,-4\). The two fractions are reciprocals, so let \(u=\frac{x+4}{x-2}\):

\[7+u=2-\frac{4}{u} \;\Rightarrow\; u^2+5u+4=0 \;\Rightarrow\; (u+1)(u+4)=0\]

If \(u=-1\): \(x+4=-(x-2)\), so \(x=-1\).

If \(u=-4\): \(x+4=-4(x-2)\), so \(5x=4\).

\(x=-1\) or \(x=\dfrac{4}{5}\)

\[2x^2-4x-1=0\]

Every problem on these sheets is built from four small tools. Most of the work is choosing which ones, and in what order.

The checklist

Almost every one of these problems is “find a slope, find a point, write the equation.” Work through in order.

  1. Is the line horizontal or vertical? Stop here. Horizontal is \(y=\) the y-coordinate; vertical is \(x=\) the x-coordinate.
  2. What slope do you have? Given outright, or from two points \(m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\), or from an equation \(Ax+By=C\) as \(m=-\frac{A}{B}\).
  3. Does it say perpendicular or parallel? Perpendicular flips the slope and changes its sign. Parallel keeps it.
  4. What point do you have? If none is given outright, the problem is telling you to build one: a midpoint, a point that partitions a segment, or the intersection of two lines.
  5. Write point-slope: \(y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\). Always start here once you have a slope and a point.
  6. Was standard form asked for? Multiply through by the denominator of the slope, move the x term left, and make A positive.
  7. Was a distance asked for? Two points go straight into the distance formula. A point and a line need the perpendicular built first.

A ratio like 7:12 or 5:4 is the signal to build a point, not to divide anything yet.

The seven patterns

Slope from two points

Cue: Two coordinate pairs and no slope given.

Method: \(m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\), kept as a fraction. Subtract in the same order top and bottom.

“Passes through the origin” hands you a free second point, \((0,0)\).

Run first, then rise: 4 over 6, so m = 2/3. Go the other way and both flip sign — −4 over −6 — which is the same fraction. That is why the order only has to match.

Worked example: GL2 #2.

Perpendicular and parallel slopes

Cue: The words perpendicular or parallel, or the \(\perp\) symbol.

Method: Perpendicular flips the fraction and changes the sign. \(-\frac52\to\frac25\), \(3\to-\frac13\), \(1\to-1\). Parallel keeps the same slope.

To pull a slope out of \(Ax+By=C\), either solve for \(y\) or use \(m=-\frac{A}{B}\) directly.

The same triangle turned a quarter turn: over 3 / up 2 becomes over 2 / up 3, and “over” now points the other way. Swapping the legs is the flip; the change of direction is the minus sign.

You only need the slope of the other line, so its constant is decoration. In GL2 #4 the 32.9 never gets used.

One pair the flip-and-negate rule cannot express: a horizontal line (slope 0) and a vertical line (no slope) are perpendicular, but 0 has no reciprocal. Handle those by sight.

Worked example: GL2 #4.

The three forms

Cue: “Write in standard form,” or an answer blank labeled Standard.

\[y-y_1=m(x-x_1) \qquad y=mx+b \qquad Ax+By=C\]

Method: Which form you start in depends on what you were handed. A slope with the y-intercept drops straight into \(y=mx+b\). A slope with any other point goes into point-slope, which is the workbench: you can write it the moment you have a slope and a point.

From either one, to reach standard form: multiply both sides by the slope’s denominator, move the x term to the left, and if A came out negative multiply every term by \(-1\).

Standard form means A positive, and no fractions or decimals anywhere.

Worked example: GL2 #1.

Midpoint

Cue: “Midpoint,” “bisects,” or “perpendicular bisector.”

\[M=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\]

Method: Average each coordinate. A perpendicular bisector is just two patterns stacked: negative reciprocal for the slope, midpoint for the point.

Worked example: GL2 #5.

Partitioning a segment by a ratio

Cue: A point placed along a segment by a ratio, as in “Q lies on \(\overline{AB}\) such that \(AQ:AB=7:12\).”

Method: Find the run and rise of the whole segment, scale both by the same fraction, and add them to the starting point.

Which fraction? That depends on what the ratio is comparing, and there are two kinds. Check whether the second name in the ratio is the whole segment or just another piece of it.

Part to whole. In \(AQ:AB=7:12\), AB is the entire segment, so the 12 already counts the whole thing. Q sits \(\frac{7}{12}\) of the way from A, and \(\frac{7}{12}\) is the number you scale by.

Part to part. In \(JP:PK=5:4\), both JP and PK are pieces, and neither is the whole. Add them to get the whole: \(5+4=9\) parts. So P sits \(\frac59\) of the way from J to K — and if you already know J and P and want K, the step from P to K is \(\frac45\) of the step from J to P, because PK is 4 parts to JP’s 5.

A ratio to the whole segment and a ratio between two pieces look almost identical. AB is the whole; PK is not.

Same idea, different denominator: the 12 is handed to you, the 9 you have to build from 5 + 4.

The teacher’s version draws a right triangle on the segment and solves \(\frac{n}{36}=\frac{7}{12}\). Same arithmetic, shown geometrically.

Scaling gives you the change from the start point, not the answer. You still have to add it on.

Worked examples: HW1 #1 and #2.

Distance between two points

Cue: Two points and the word distance, or a length you need before anything else can be answered.

\[D=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\]

It is the Pythagorean theorem. The two differences are the legs, the distance is the hypotenuse, and \(a^2+b^2=c^2\) is the formula.

Method: Squaring removes the signs, so subtraction order doesn’t matter here — unlike in the slope formula.

Then simplify the radical: split the number into a perfect square times whatever is left over, and take the root of the square. \(\sqrt{50}=\sqrt{25\cdot2}=5\sqrt2\).

Some won’t simplify at all. HW1 #2 finishes at \(\sqrt{6266}\), and since \(6266=2\cdot13\cdot241\) has no repeated factor, there is no square to pull out. It stays exactly as it is — that is a finished answer, not an unfinished one.

Worked example: HW1 #2.

Distance from a point to a line

Cue: A point and a line, and the word distance.

This is the four-step one. Distance means the shortest distance, which runs perpendicular to the line, so you have to find where that perpendicular lands before you can measure anything.

  1. Take the slope of the given line and flip it to the perpendicular slope.
  2. Write the perpendicular line through the given point.
  3. Set the two equations equal and solve. That x, with its y, is the foot of the perpendicular.
  4. Distance formula from the original point to the foot.

Every other route from A to the line is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose leg is the red segment, so it can only be longer. That is why the perpendicular has to be built before anything can be measured.

The foot is nearly always an ugly fraction. Keep it as a fraction — decimals will wreck the radical at the end.

Why the answers come out looking like \(\frac{23\sqrt{13}}{13}\). The foot’s two coordinates share a denominator — 13 in DPL #1, 41 in the other three — so both differences do too. Squaring turns that denominator into a perfect square, and the root of a perfect square is a whole number:

\[\sqrt{\frac{6877}{169}}=\frac{\sqrt{6877}}{13}=\frac{23\sqrt{13}}{13}\]

So the bottom is left with no radical on it and the answer is already rationalized. Nothing further is needed.

Worked examples: all four of DPL.

HW1 #1Partition + perpendicular

Point Q lies on \(\overline{AB}\) such that \(AQ:AB=7:12\). Find the equation of the line perpendicular to \(\overline{AB}\) through Q, in standard form. \(A(-80,-6)\) and \(B(4,30)\).

Solution

Cue: A ratio along a segment, then the word perpendicular.

Method: Partition to build Q, negative reciprocal for the slope, point-slope, then convert to standard.

AB in green with its 84-by-36 triangle; the red 49-and-21 step lands on Q, and the blue line is the answer.

Run and rise of the whole segment.

\[\Delta x = 4-(-80)=84 \qquad \Delta y = 30-(-6)=36\]

Scale by the ratio. \(AQ:AB=7:12\) is part-to-whole, so Q sits \(\frac{7}{12}\) of the way from A:

\[\tfrac{7}{12}(84)=49 \qquad \tfrac{7}{12}(36)=21\]

Add to A, since those are changes, not coordinates:

\[Q=(-80+49,\ -6+21)=(-31,15)\]

Slopes.

\[m_{AB}=\frac{36}{84}=\frac{3}{7} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_\perp=-\frac{7}{3}\]

Point-slope, then standard. Multiply by 3 to clear the denominator:

\[y-15=-\tfrac73(x+31) \;\Rightarrow\; 3y-45=-7x-217\]
\(7x+3y=-172\)
HW1 #2Partition + distance

Point P lies on \(\overline{JK}\) such that \(JP:PK=5:4\), with \(J(-10,10)\) and \(P(35,-15)\). Find the distance from point K to the origin.

Solution

Cue: A ratio again, but here you’re given the middle point and asked for the far end.

Method: Scale the known piece to get the unknown piece, add it to P, then use the distance formula.

J to P in green, the scaled PK step in red, and the dashed distance from K back to the origin.

Run and rise from J to P.

\[\Delta x = 35-(-10)=45 \qquad \Delta y = -15-10=-25\]

Scale to get PK. This ratio is part-to-part: \(JP:PK=5:4\), so PK is \(\frac45\) of JP.

\[\tfrac45(45)=36 \qquad \tfrac45(-25)=-20\]

Continue past P, in the same direction:

\[K=(35+36,\ -15-20)=(71,-35)\]

Distance to the origin.

\[D=\sqrt{(71-0)^2+(-35-0)^2}=\sqrt{5041+1225}=\sqrt{6266}\]

\(6266=2\cdot13\cdot241\), with no perfect-square factor, so the radical doesn’t simplify.

\(D=\sqrt{6266}\approx79.2\)

The key adds 5041 + 1225 and writes √6226, but that sum is 6266.

DPL #1Point to line

Distance from \(A(-4,1)\) to \(y=\frac23x-4\).

Solution

Cue: A point, a line, and the word distance.

Method: Perpendicular slope, perpendicular line through the point, intersection, distance formula.

The given line in blue, the perpendicular dropped from the point in red. That red segment is the distance.

1. Perpendicular slope. \(\frac23\to-\frac32\).

2. Perpendicular line through A.

\[y-1=-\tfrac32(x+4) \;\Rightarrow\; y=-\tfrac32x-5\]

3. Where they cross. Set the two right sides equal and multiply by 6:

\[-\tfrac32x-5=\tfrac23x-4 \;\Rightarrow\; -9x-30=4x-24 \;\Rightarrow\; -13x=6\]
\[x=-\tfrac{6}{13} \qquad y=\tfrac23\!\left(-\tfrac{6}{13}\right)-4=-\tfrac{4}{13}-\tfrac{52}{13}=-\tfrac{56}{13}\]

The foot is \(\left(-\frac{6}{13},-\frac{56}{13}\right)\).

4. Distance from A to the foot. Put each difference over 13 first:

\[D=\sqrt{\left(-\tfrac{52}{13}+\tfrac{6}{13}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{13}{13}+\tfrac{56}{13}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{2116+4761}{169}}=\sqrt{\frac{6877}{169}}\]

Simplify. \(6877=13\cdot529=13\cdot23^2\), so \(\sqrt{6877}=23\sqrt{13}\), and \(\sqrt{169}=13\).

\(D=\dfrac{23\sqrt{13}}{13}\approx6.38\)
DPL #2Point to line

Distance from \(B(5,0)\) to \(y+2=-\frac45(x+4)\).

Solution

Cue: Same as #1, but the line arrives in point-slope form.

Method: Rewrite it as \(y=mx+b\) first, then the same four steps.

The given line in blue, the perpendicular dropped from the point in red. That red segment is the distance.

0. Tidy the given line.

\[y=-\tfrac45x-\tfrac{16}{5}-2=-\tfrac45x-\tfrac{26}{5}\]

1–2. Perpendicular slope \(\frac54\), through B.

\[y-0=\tfrac54(x-5) \;\Rightarrow\; y=\tfrac54x-\tfrac{25}{4}\]

3. Intersection. Multiply by 20 to clear both denominators at once:

\[25x-125=-16x-104 \;\Rightarrow\; 41x=21 \;\Rightarrow\; x=\tfrac{21}{41}\]
\[y=\tfrac54\!\left(\tfrac{21}{41}\right)-\tfrac{25}{4}=\tfrac{105}{164}-\tfrac{1025}{164}=-\tfrac{920}{164}=-\tfrac{230}{41}\]

4. Distance.

\[D=\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{205}{41}-\tfrac{21}{41}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{230}{41}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{33856+52900}{1681}}=\sqrt{\frac{86756}{1681}}\]

\(86756=4\cdot41\cdot23^2\), so \(\sqrt{86756}=2\cdot23\sqrt{41}=46\sqrt{41}\), and \(\sqrt{1681}=41\).

\(D=\dfrac{46\sqrt{41}}{41}\approx7.18\)
DPL #3Point to line

Distance from \(A(-5,-2)\) to \(4x+5y=20\).

Solution

Cue: Same again, with the line in standard form.

Method: Solve for y (or read \(m=-\frac{A}{B}\)), then the same four steps.

The given line in blue, the perpendicular dropped from the point in red. That red segment is the distance.

0. Solve for y. \(5y=-4x+20\), so \(y=-\frac45x+4\).

1–2. Perpendicular slope \(\frac54\), through A.

\[y+2=\tfrac54(x+5) \;\Rightarrow\; y=\tfrac54x+\tfrac{25}{4}-2=\tfrac54x+\tfrac{17}{4}\]

3. Intersection. Multiply by 20:

\[25x+85=-16x+80 \;\Rightarrow\; 41x=-5 \;\Rightarrow\; x=-\tfrac{5}{41}\]
\[y=-\tfrac45\!\left(-\tfrac{5}{41}\right)+4=\tfrac{4}{41}+\tfrac{164}{41}=\tfrac{168}{41}\]

4. Distance.

\[D=\sqrt{\left(-\tfrac{205}{41}+\tfrac{5}{41}\right)^2+\left(-\tfrac{82}{41}-\tfrac{168}{41}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{40000+62500}{1681}}=\sqrt{\frac{102500}{1681}}\]

\(102500=2500\cdot41\), so \(\sqrt{102500}=50\sqrt{41}\).

\(D=\dfrac{50\sqrt{41}}{41}\approx7.81\)
DPL #4Point to line

Distance from \(B(4,4)\) to \(2x+6y=12\).

Solution

Cue: Same four steps, but this one is built to come out clean.

Method: Solve for y, perpendicular through B, intersect, distance.

The given line in blue, the perpendicular dropped from the point in red. That red segment is the distance.

0. Solve for y. \(6y=-2x+12\), so \(y=-\frac13x+2\).

1–2. Perpendicular slope 3, through B.

\[y-4=3(x-4) \;\Rightarrow\; y=3x-8\]

Solution

Cue: A slope and a y-intercept is exactly what \(y=mx+b\) takes.

GL2 #2Two points

Passes through \(A\!\left(4,\frac{15}{4}\right)\) and the origin.

Solution

Cue: No slope given, but “the origin” is a second point, \((0,0)\).

Method: Slope formula, then clear denominators.

The answer line on the worksheet grid.

\[m=\frac{\frac{15}{4}-0}{4-0}=\frac{15}{4}\cdot\frac14=\frac{15}{16}\]

Through the origin, b is 0, so \(y=\frac{15}{16}x\). Multiply by 16:

\[16y=15x\]
\(15x-16y=0\)

C = 0 is a fine answer. Write 15x − 16y, not −15x + 16y, so A stays positive.

GL2 #4Perpendicular

Passes through \(D(-8,7)\) and is perpendicular to \(5x+2y=32.9\).

Solution

Cue: Perpendicular to a line given in standard form.

Method: Read its slope as \(-\frac{A}{B}\), flip it, then point-slope.

Perpendicular to \(\overline{AB}\), with \(A(-8,-3)\) and \(B(4,9)\), passing through \(\overline{AB}\)’s midpoint.
